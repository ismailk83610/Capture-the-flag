
# Frontier-Based Exploration
## Literature Research and Application to the Capture the Flag Agent

# 1. Introduction

Frontier-Based Exploration is an autonomous exploration method used in robotics and intelligent agents to explore an unknown environment.

The main idea is to identify the boundary between already explored space and unexplored space, and then move toward that boundary to discover new areas.

In our Capture the Flag project, the agent starts without complete knowledge of the game environment.

The agent must:

1. Explore the unknown environment.
2. Identify walls and traversable paths.
3. Store discovered information in an internal map.
4. Avoid unnecessary repeated exploration.
5. Search for the enemy flag.
6. Capture the flag.
7. Return to its home position.

Frontier-Based Exploration is being investigated as the main exploration algorithm.

It will eventually be combined with:

- Dynamic Map Storage
- Information Gain
- A* Pathfinding
- Finite State Machine (FSM)

**Important:** Frontier-Based Exploration determines where the agent should explore. It does not automatically determine the shortest path or guarantee the fastest flag discovery.

---

# 2. Understanding the Environment

The environment is represented as a two-dimensional grid.

Each grid cell can have different properties, such as:

- Traversable floor
- Wall or obstacle
- Flag
- Home position
- Other game-specific objects

For exploration, the map must distinguish between three basic knowledge states.

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| UNKNOWN | The agent has not observed the cell |
| FREE | The agent knows the cell is traversable |
| BLOCKED | The agent knows the cell cannot be traversed |

These knowledge states are separate from game-specific properties such as flags, bases, or special terrain.

## 2.1 Example of an Unknown Map

```text
? ? ? ? ? ? ?
? ? ? ? ? ? ?
? ? . . . ? ?
? ? . A . ? ?
? ? . . . ? ?
? ? ? ? ? ? ?
? ? ? ? ? ? ?
```

Symbols:

- `A` = Agent
- `.` = Known traversable cell
- `?` = Unknown cell
- `#` = Known wall

The agent knows only the cells that it has already observed.

The purpose of exploration is to progressively convert unknown cells into known cells.

---

# 3. What Is a Frontier?

A frontier is a known, traversable cell that is adjacent to at least one unknown cell.

In simple words:

> A frontier is the boundary between known free space and unexplored space.

The frontier cell itself is already known.

It is not an unknown cell.

## 3.1 Example

```text
? ? ? ? ?
? . . ? ?
? . F ? ?
? . . ? ?
? ? ? ? ?
```

Here:

- `F` is a known free cell.
- The cell to its right is unknown.
- Therefore, `F` is a frontier.

The agent can select this frontier as an exploration target.

## 3.2 Important Properties

1. A frontier must be a known traversable cell.
2. It must be adjacent to at least one unknown cell.
3. A known wall is not a frontier.
4. A frontier can become an ordinary known cell after further observations.
5. New frontiers can appear as the known map expands.
6. Frontier cells must be checked for reachability before being used as navigation targets.



# 5. The 5 × 5 Observation Window

For our current project design, we are working with a 5 × 5 local observation window.

This observation model should be verified against the exact definitions in `agent_api.h` before implementation.

The agent is located at the center of the window.

The window extends:

- Two cells north
- Two cells south
- Two cells east
- Two cells west

## 5.1 Observation Example

```text
? ? ? ? ? ? ?
? O O O O O ?
? O O O O O ?
? O O A O O ?
? O O O O O ?
? O O O O O ?
? ? ? ? ? ? ?
```

Symbols:

- `A` = Agent
- `O` = Cell inside the observation window
- `?` = Cell outside the observation window

The window contains:

\[
5\times5=25\text{ cells}
\]

Assuming all 25 cells are reported by the API, the agent receives information about its current cell and the surrounding 24 cells.

This does not necessarily mean all cells are traversable.

Some may be walls or other terrain types.

## 5.2 What Happens When the Agent Moves?

Suppose the agent moves one cell east.

Its observation window also moves one cell east.

This can reveal a new column of five cells.

The cells from the previous observation remain stored in the internal map.

Therefore:

**The agent can discover new cells before physically reaching its selected frontier.**

This is especially important for our project.

The agent does not need to move all the way to a frontier before obtaining new information.

# 6. Frontier Exploration With a 5 × 5 Window

Consider a simplified example with no obstacles.

## Step 1: Initial Observation

```text
? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ?
? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ?
? ? . . . . . ? ?
? ? . . . . . ? ?
? ? . . A . . ? ?
? ? . . . . . ? ?
? ? . . . . . ? ?
? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ?
? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ?
```

The agent initially observes a 5 × 5 region.

The free cells along the boundary of this region are frontier cells because they are adjacent to unknown cells.

## Step 2: Frontier Detection

We mark frontier cells with `F`.

```text
? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ?
? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ?
? ? F F F F F ? ?
? ? F . . . F ? ?
? ? F . A . F ? ?
? ? F . . . F ? ?
? ? F F F F F ? ?
? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ?
? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ?
```

The agent now has multiple possible exploration targets.

The frontier algorithm identifies these candidates.

It does not yet decide which one is best.

## Step 3: Select a Frontier

Suppose the agent chooses a frontier on the eastern boundary.

It calculates a path toward that frontier.

For example:

```text
A -> . -> F
```

The agent begins moving east.

## Step 4: Discover Cells While Moving

After the agent moves one cell east, its observation window shifts.

A new column of cells becomes visible.

The internal map is updated.

Some old frontier cells may no longer border unknown cells.

New frontier cells appear along the expanded boundary.

## Step 5: Recalculate Frontiers

After receiving new observations, the agent:

1. Updates the stored map.
2. Rechecks frontier cells.
3. Determines whether its current target remains useful.
4. Continues toward the target or selects a new one.

The agent should not change targets unnecessarily.

Frequent target switching may increase travel distance and cause oscillation.

## Step 6: Repeat

The process continues:

```text
Observe
   |
   v
Update Map
   |
   v
Detect Frontiers
   |
   v
Select Frontier
   |
   v
Plan Path
   |
   v
Move
   |
   v
Observe Again
```

This cycle continues until the agent finds the flag, finishes exploration, or encounters another condition requiring a different behavior.

# 7. Frontier Detection

Frontier detection is the process of finding all frontier cells in the stored map.

For every known traversable cell:

1. Check its north neighbor.
2. Check its south neighbor.
3. Check its east neighbor.
4. Check its west neighbor.
5. If at least one neighbor is unknown, classify the cell as a frontier.




---

# 8. Frontier Detection vs. Frontier Selection

These are two separate operations.

## 8.1 Frontier Detection

Find all frontier cells.

Example:

```text
Frontiers = {F1, F2, F3, F4}
```

## 8.2 Frontier Selection

Choose the next frontier to explore.

Possible selection methods include:

- Nearest frontier
- Random frontier
- Frontier with maximum expected information gain
- Frontier selected using information gain and travel cost

Our project will investigate combining Frontier Exploration with Information Gain.

---

# 9. Nearest-Frontier Selection

The simplest strategy is to select the nearest reachable frontier.

Suppose:

| Frontier | Travel distance |
|---|---|
| F1 | 3 steps |
| F2 | 7 steps |
| F3 | 5 steps |

The agent selects F1.

Mathematically:

\[
f^*=\arg\min_{f\in F_r}d(A,f)
\]

Where:

- \(F_r\) = Set of reachable frontiers
- \(A\) = Current agent position
- \(d(A,f)\) = Shortest path cost from the agent to frontier \(f\)

The distance should be calculated through traversable cells.

Straight-line distance alone may be misleading when walls are present.

## 9.1 Advantages

- Simple to implement.
- Low decision-making complexity.
- Can reduce travel to the next exploration target.

## 9.2 Limitations

- The nearest frontier may reveal very little new information.
- It may lead the agent into small unexplored regions.
- It does not consider where the enemy flag is likely to be.
- It may require repeated travel between different areas.

Therefore, nearest-frontier exploration is a useful baseline for evaluating more advanced approaches.

# 12. What Happens When a Wall Is Discovered?

Suppose the agent selects a frontier.

Initially, its planned path is:

```text
A . . . F
```

After moving, it discovers a wall:

```text
A . # . F
```

The original path may no longer be valid.

The agent should:

1. Record the obstacle in the map.
2. Check whether the current route remains valid.
3. Calculate an alternative path if necessary.
4. Check whether the target frontier is still reachable.
5. Select another frontier if the original target cannot be reached.

This is why Frontier Exploration must work with a pathfinding algorithm.

Frontier Exploration answers:

**Where should the agent explore?**

Pathfinding answers:

**How can the agent reach that location?**

Our proposed pathfinding algorithm is A*.

# 16. Advantages of Frontier-Based Exploration

## 16.1 Suitable for Unknown Environments

The agent does not need the complete map at the beginning.

It can gradually explore using local observations.

## 16.2 Systematic Exploration

The algorithm explicitly targets the boundary of known and unknown space.

This is more structured than choosing completely random directions.

## 16.3 Compatible With Map Storage

The method naturally uses the map that the agent builds during exploration.

## 16.4 Can Be Combined With Other Algorithms

Frontier Exploration can be combined with:

- Information Gain
- A*
- Dynamic replanning
- Finite State Machines

## 16.5 Suitable for Grid-Based Environments

The method can be implemented using simple grid operations and neighbor checks.

This makes it a reasonable candidate for a C++ DLL agent.

---

# 17. Limitations of Frontier-Based Exploration

## 17.1 Does Not Guarantee Fast Flag Discovery

The goal of our project is not simply to explore the entire map.

It is to find and capture the enemy flag.

A frontier strategy may explore regions that do not contain the flag.

## 17.2 Repeated Movement

The agent may need to travel through previously explored areas to reach other frontiers.

## 17.3 Frontier Selection Matters

Selecting the nearest frontier is not always the most efficient exploration strategy.

## 17.4 Dynamic Obstacles

If the map changes, previously planned routes may become invalid.

## 17.5 Unreachable Frontiers

Some detected frontiers may not be reachable from the agent's current position.

Reachability checks are necessary.

## 17.6 Dependence on Observation Rules

The algorithm's behavior depends on the information available through the game API.

A 5 × 5 observation window may allow the agent to discover cells before reaching its frontier target.

However, the exact observation and visibility rules must be confirmed.

---


# 23. Preliminary Decision

Based on the literature and our current project requirements, we have selected Frontier-Based Exploration as the preliminary exploration approach.

We plan to investigate it together with Information Gain.

### Reasons for Selection

- The environment is initially unknown.
- The agent receives local observations.
- The agent must build a map while exploring.
- Frontier detection can be implemented using grid operations.
- The method is compatible with dynamic map storage.
- It can be combined with A* for navigation.
- It provides a structured baseline for experimental comparison.
